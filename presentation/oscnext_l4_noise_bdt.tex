% =========================================================== 2  oscNEXT ====
\begin{frame}{oscNext and where L4 sits}
  \framesubtitle{low-energy neutrino selection in DeepCore}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      \textbf{oscNext} is the low-energy ($\sim$5--100\,GeV) neutrino sample used
      for the DeepCore oscillation measurements.  The selection runs as a chain
      of levels, each one cheaper than the reconstruction that follows it.

      \medskip
      \begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{L2} & DeepCore Filter: fiducial vs.\ veto \\
        \textbf{L3} & fast cuts, veto variables \\
        \textbf{\textcolor{bad}{L4}} & \textbf{two BDTs: noise, muon} \\
        L5--L7 & reconstruction, PID, final cuts \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}

      \medskip
      At L3 the sample is still dominated by background:
      atmospheric muons at $\sim$505\,mHz and pure detector noise at
      $\sim$37\,mHz, against $\sim$5\,mHz of neutrinos.

      \medskip
      \textbf{L4 is where that ratio is inverted}, and it has to do so
      without the expensive reconstruction that only starts at L5.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
      \centering
      \plot[width=0.95\linewidth]{oscnext_levels.png}
      \\[2pt]
      {\scriptsize oscNext selection chain / DeepCore geometry}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{Set the scene: L4 is a background-rejection step, not a reconstruction
  step.  The whole point is that it must be cheap.}
\end{frame}

% =========================================================== 4  L4 ========
\begin{frame}{What L4 does, and what had to be rebuilt}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      Two independent classifiers, applied as a conjunction:
      \[
        \text{L4} \;=\; P_{\nu}^{\text{noise}} \ge 0.70
        \;\wedge\;
        P_{\nu}^{\text{muon}} \ge 0.65
      \]

      \medskip
      \textbf{Why it needed rebuilding.}  The original L4 tray segment is
      commented out in full and depends on three projects absent from the
      current meta-project:
      \begin{itemize}\footnotesize
        \item \texttt{icecube.oscNext} -- no \texttt{I3Classifier}
        \item \texttt{tau\_bdt} -- VICH
        \item \texttt{analysis.event\_selection} -- Dunkman variables
      \end{itemize}

      \medskip
      So 5 of the 14 BDT inputs are \textbf{rewritten in pure Python} from
      their definitions in the technical note.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \centering\footnotesize
      \textbf{Where the 14 inputs come from}\\[4pt]
      \begin{tabular}{@{}clp{3.3cm}@{}}
        \toprule
        n & source & risk \\
        \midrule
        4 & ready from L3      & validated \\
        5 & IceTray projects   & validated \\
        \textbf{5} & \textbf{our rewrite} & \textcolor{bad}{unverified} \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}

      \medskip
      \raggedright
      \textbf{All of the risk is in the last row}, and it is why the
      validation against the note (and against the original pass2 code)
      was done line by line.

      \medskip
      That comparison found four bugs; three of them produced
      \textbf{silent} data corruption -- wrong numbers, no error.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{Do not dwell on the bug list here -- one sentence.  If asked: index
  table overwriting data, non-unique event IDs mismatching rows,
  n\_flux\_events frozen per file, and the micro\_count cleaning step that was
  bypassed (inherited from the original pass2 code).}
\end{frame}

% =========================================================== 6  BDT =======
\begin{frame}{How a boosted decision tree works}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      A single shallow tree is a weak classifier.  \textbf{Boosting} trains
      many of them in sequence, each one correcting what the previous ones got
      wrong, and sums their outputs.

      \medskip
      The two engines differ in \emph{how} the next tree is chosen:

      \medskip
      \begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.2cm}p{3.6cm}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{AdaBoost}\newline(pybdt) &
        re-weights the \emph{events} the ensemble misclassifies \\[4pt]
        \textbf{Gradient}\newline\textbf{boosting}\newline(LightGBM) &
        fits the next tree to the \emph{gradient} of the loss \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}

      \medskip
      The technical note (\S3.6.1) uses LightGBM, and Table 10's
      hyperparameters are its native names.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \centering
      \plot[width=0.92\linewidth]{bdt_schematic.png}
      \\[4pt]
      \footnotesize
      \raggedright
      \textbf{The output.}  An event is passed through every tree; the leaf
      values are summed and squashed into $P(\text{signal}) \in [0,1]$.

      \smallskip
      This is why the note's cut of 0.70 transfers directly to a LightGBM
      model -- and why it does \emph{not} transfer to a pybdt score, which
      lives on $[-1,1]$.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{Keep this slide short -- the audience knows what a BDT is.  The one
  point that matters downstream is the output scale, because it decides
  whether the note's cut value can be reused.}
\end{frame}

      \scriptsize
      \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
        \toprule
        model & 90\,\% & 95\,\% & 99\,\% \\
        \midrule
        stump (d1)  & 91.3 & 76.0 & 76.0$^*$ \\
        \textbf{d2t500} & \textbf{94.0} & \textbf{91.7} & 62.0 \\
        d3t300      & 88.9 & 75.2 & 43.7 \\

        d6t500      & 13.4 & 10.3 &  5.8 \\
        \bottomrule
        \multicolumn{4}{@{}l}{signal efficiency [\%] at fixed noise rejection}\\
        \multicolumn{4}{@{}l}{$^*$depth-1 ensemble: too few distinct scores,}\\
        \multicolumn{4}{@{}l}{\phantom{$^*$}a coarse staircase, drops to 0 at 99.5\%}
      \end{tabular}

      \medskip
      \raggedright\footnotesize
      \textbf{Best: 65.8\,\% at 99\,\% rejection.}  The target is $\sim$96\,\%.

      \medskip
      \textbf{Two things measured along the way:}

      \smallskip
      \tick~Training is \emph{deterministic} -- the same configuration twice
      gives bit-identical scores.

      \smallskip
      \cross~The KS $p$-value is \textbf{useless} as an overtraining test
      here: it flagged the best model ($p=0.002$) and cleared the worst
      ($p=0.98$).  We use the train/test efficiency \emph{gap} instead.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{The honest framing: the pybdt hyperparameters were hand-picked, not
  tuned to the same effort as Table 10.  So this slide is not "AdaBoost is a
  worse algorithm" -- it is "our pybdt setup costs us a lot".}
\end{frame}

% =========================================================== 9  MC LIMIT ==
\begin{frame}{The limit: noise MC statistics}
  \framesubtitle{why nothing right of 99\,\% rejection is a measurement}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      100 L3 files give \textbf{2\,051} noise events -- 1\,035 train,
      1\,016 test -- against \textbf{329\,545} signal events.
      A ratio of \textbf{159:1}.

      \medskip
      \begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
        \toprule
        rejection & bg events left \\
        \midrule
        90.0\,\% & 101 \\
        95.0\,\% & 50 \\
        99.0\,\% & \textcolor{warn}{10} \\
        99.5\,\% & \textcolor{bad}{5} \\
        99.9\,\% & \textcolor{bad}{1} \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}

      \medskip
      The Table 13 target sits at \textbf{99.2\,\%} rejection -- which our
      test set defines with \textbf{8 events}.

      \medskip
      Table 10's \texttt{min\_data\_in\_leaf}$=500$ is itself evidence that the
      reference had far more: with 1\,035 training events that setting would
      allow two leaves.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \centering
      \plot[width=\linewidth]{lightgbm_cuts.png}
      \\[1pt]
      {\scriptsize grey lines: where 100 / 10 / 1 background events remain}
      \\[3pt]
      \plot[width=0.88\linewidth]{mc_scaling.png}
      \\[1pt]
      {\scriptsize efficiency at fixed rejection vs.\ background sample size --
       is the trend still rising?}

      \medskip
      \raggedright\footnotesize
      \textbf{It is not the bottleneck it looked like.}  An earlier conclusion
      held that the background statistics limited the model; the LightGBM
      measurement retracted that -- 1\,035 events are enough to reach the
      target.

      \smallskip
      What they are \emph{not} enough for is \textbf{measuring} the region
      beyond 99\,\%.  More vuvuzela MC is valuable, but it is not blocking.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{Be precise here: more MC is needed to measure the high-rejection
  region, not to reach the target.  Saying it the other way round would
  overstate the request.}
\end{frame}
