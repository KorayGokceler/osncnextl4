      \textbf{oscNext} is the low-energy ($\sim$5--100\,GeV) neutrino sample

      \medskip
      \begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{L2} & DeepCore Filter: fiducial vs.\ veto \\
        \textbf{L3} & fast cuts, veto variables \\

        L5--L7 & reconstruction, PID, final cuts \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}

      \medskip
      At L3 the sample is still background dominated: atmospheric muons at
      $\sim$505\,mHz and detector noise at $\sim$37\,mHz, against $\sim$5\,mHz
      of neutrinos.

      \medskip
      \textbf{L4 inverts that ratio} -- and has to do it without the
      reconstruction that only starts at L5.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \centering
      \plot{oscnext_levels.png}
      \figcap{oscNext selection chain / DeepCore geometry}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{Set the scene: L4 is background rejection, not reconstruction.  The
  whole point is that it must be cheap.}
\end{frame}

% =========================================================== 4  L4 ========
\begin{frame}{What L4 does, and what had to be rebuilt}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      Two independent classifiers, applied as a conjunction:
      \[
        \text{L4} \;=\; P_{\nu}^{\text{noise}} \ge 0.70
        \;\wedge\;
        P_{\nu}^{\text{muon}} \ge 0.65
      \]

      \medskip
      \textbf{Why it needed rebuilding.}  The original L4 tray segment is
      commented out in full and depends on three projects absent from the
      current meta-project:
      \begin{itemize}\footnotesize
        \item \texttt{icecube.oscNext} -- no \texttt{I3Classifier}
        \item \texttt{tau\_bdt} -- VICH
        \item \texttt{analysis.event\_selection} -- Dunkman variables
      \end{itemize}

      \medskip
      So 5 of the 14 BDT inputs are \textbf{rewritten in pure Python} from
      their definitions in the technical note.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \centering\footnotesize
      \textbf{Where the 14 inputs come from}\\[5pt]
      \begin{tabular}{@{}clp{2.9cm}@{}}
        \toprule
        n & source & status \\
        \midrule
        4 & ready from L3    & validated \\
        5 & IceTray projects & validated \\
        \textbf{5} & \textbf{our rewrite} & \textcolor{bad}{unverified} \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}

      \medskip
      \raggedright
      \textbf{All of the risk is in the last row.}  That is why the code was
      compared line by line against the note \emph{and} against the original
      pass2 segment.

      \medskip
      That comparison found four bugs.  Three produced \textbf{silent} data
      corruption -- wrong numbers, no error raised.  One of them,
      \texttt{micro\_count} counted without noise cleaning, turned out to be
      inherited from the original pass2 code rather than introduced by us.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{One sentence on the bugs; do not enumerate.  If asked: the index table
  overwriting data, non-unique event IDs mismatching rows, n\_flux\_events
  frozen across files, and the bypassed cleaning step in micro\_count.}
\end{frame}

% =========================================================== 6  BDT =======
\setlength{\plotht}{0.44\textheight}
\begin{frame}{How a boosted decision tree works}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      A single shallow tree is a weak classifier.  \textbf{Boosting} trains
      many in sequence, each correcting what the previous ones got wrong, and
      sums their outputs.

      \medskip
      The two engines differ in \emph{how} the next tree is chosen:

      \medskip
      \begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.0cm}p{3.4cm}@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{AdaBoost}\newline(pybdt) &
        re-weights the \emph{events} the ensemble gets wrong \\[4pt]
        \textbf{Gradient}\newline\textbf{boosting}\newline(LightGBM) &
        fits the next tree to the \emph{gradient} of the loss \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}

      \medskip
      The note (\S3.6.1) uses LightGBM, and the Table 10 hyperparameters are
      its native names.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \centering
      \plot{bdt_schematic.png}
      \figcap{trees in sequence, leaf values summed}

      \medskip
      \raggedright\footnotesize
      \textbf{The output.}  An event passes through every tree; the leaf
      values are summed and squashed into $P(\text{signal}) \in [0,1]$.

      \smallskip
      This is why the note's cut of 0.70 transfers directly to a LightGBM
      model -- and why it does \emph{not} transfer to a pybdt score, which
      lives on $[-1,1]$.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{Keep this short -- the audience knows what a BDT is.  The one point
  that matters downstream is the output scale, because it decides whether the
  note's cut value can be reused.}
\end{frame}

% =========================================================== 7  PYBDT =====
\setlength{\plotht}{0.68\textheight}
\begin{frame}{Training with pybdt (AdaBoost)}
  \framesubtitle{a deliberate deviation from the note -- and its cost}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
      \centering
      \plot{pybdt_model_comparison.png}
      \figcap{six configurations, efficiency vs.\ rejection, test set}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \scriptsize
      \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
        \toprule
        model & 90\,\% & 95\,\% & 99\,\% \\
        \midrule
        stump (d1)      & 91.3 & 76.0 & 76.0$^*$ \\
        \textbf{d2t500} & \textbf{94.0} & \textbf{91.7} & 62.0 \\
        d3t300          & 88.9 & 75.2 & 43.7 \\
        d4t500          & 91.5 & 86.1 & \textbf{65.8} \\
        d6t500          & 13.4 & 10.3 &  5.8 \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}

      signal efficiency [\%] at fixed noise rejection.  $^*$depth-1 gives too
      few distinct scores -- a coarse staircase that drops to 0 beyond
      99.5\,\%.

      \medskip
      \footnotesize\raggedright
      \textbf{Best: 65.8\,\% at 99\,\% rejection.}  The target is
      $\sim$96\,\%.

      \medskip
      \tick~Training is \emph{deterministic} -- the same configuration twice
      gives bit-identical scores, so there is nothing to average over.

      \medskip
      \cross~The KS $p$-value is \textbf{useless} as an overtraining test
      here: it flagged the best model ($p=0.002$) and cleared the worst
      ($p=0.98$).  We use the train/test efficiency \emph{gap} instead.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{Be honest about the framing: the pybdt hyperparameters were
  hand-picked, not tuned to the same effort as Table 10.  So this is not
  "AdaBoost is a worse algorithm" -- it is "our pybdt setup costs us a lot".}
\end{frame}

% =========================================================== 9  MC LIMIT ==
\setlength{\plotht}{0.64\textheight}
\begin{frame}{The limit: noise MC statistics}
  \framesubtitle{why nothing right of 99\,\% rejection is a measurement}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      100 L3 files give \textbf{2\,051} noise events -- 1\,035 train, 1\,016
      test -- against \textbf{329\,545} signal.  A ratio of \textbf{159:1}.

      \medskip
      \begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
        \toprule
        rejection & bg events left \\
        \midrule
        90.0\,\% & 101 \\
        95.0\,\% & 50 \\
        99.0\,\% & \textcolor{warn}{10} \\
        99.5\,\% & \textcolor{bad}{5} \\
        99.9\,\% & \textcolor{bad}{1} \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}

      \medskip
      The Table 13 target sits at \textbf{99.2\,\%} rejection, which our test
      set defines with \textbf{8 events}.

      \medskip
      Table 10's \texttt{min\_data\_in\_leaf}$=500$ is itself evidence the
      reference had far more: with 1\,035 training events that setting allows
      two leaves.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \centering
      % swap for mc_scaling.png if the background-scaling scan gets produced
      \plot{lightgbm_cuts.png}
      \figcap{grey lines: where 100 / 10 / 1 background events remain}

      \medskip
      \raggedright\footnotesize
      \textbf{It is not the bottleneck it looked like.}  An earlier conclusion
      held that the background statistics limited the model; the LightGBM
      measurement retracted that -- 1\,035 events are enough to reach the
      target.

      \smallskip
      What they are \emph{not} enough for is \textbf{measuring} the region
      beyond 99\,\%.  More vuvuzela MC is valuable, but it is not blocking.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{Be precise: more MC is needed to measure the high-rejection region,
  not to reach the target.  The other way round would overstate the request.}
\end{frame}
